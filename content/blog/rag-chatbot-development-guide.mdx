Retrieval-augmented generation, usually shortened to RAG, is the most common way businesses connect a language model to their own knowledge. Done well, it produces an assistant that answers from your policies, manuals and tickets and shows its sources. Done badly, it produces a confident chatbot that quotes the wrong policy. This guide explains how RAG chatbots work, which design choices matter and how to avoid the mistakes we see most often when teams move from demo to production.

## What RAG actually is

A language model knows what it learned in training, which does not include your internal documents and may be out of date. RAG solves this by splitting the job into two steps:

1. **Retrieve.** When a user asks a question, the system searches your knowledge base and pulls back the most relevant passages.
2. **Generate.** The model receives the question and those passages in its prompt and writes an answer based on them, ideally citing which passage supported which claim.

The model is not "learning" your data. It is reading selected extracts at question time. That distinction explains most of RAG's strengths (fresh content, citations, permission control) and weaknesses (if retrieval misses the right passage, the answer will be wrong however capable the model is).

## When RAG is the right tool

RAG fits when users ask questions whose answers live in documents that change: HR and IT policy, product documentation, compliance libraries, contract repositories, support knowledge bases, research archives and engineering wikis. It is a weaker fit when the answer requires calculation across structured data (use a database query tool instead), when the corpus is tiny enough to paste into a prompt, or when the task is an action rather than a lookup. For actions, you want an agent: a system that can call tools such as "create ticket" or "fetch order status" under permissions and approvals. Most useful business assistants combine both, retrieving knowledge and then acting. Our [AI agents and RAG assistants service](/services/ai-agents-rag-assistants) covers both patterns.

## The architecture, piece by piece

### 1. Ingestion

Documents arrive as PDFs, Word files, slides, web pages, tickets and spreadsheets, often messy. Ingestion extracts text (with OCR for scans), keeps structure such as headings and tables, attaches metadata (source, owner, date, department, access group) and handles updates and deletions so the index does not serve stale or removed content. Teams underestimate this stage. If a table is flattened into gibberish at ingestion, no prompt can recover it.

### 2. Chunking

Documents are cut into passages small enough to be specific but large enough to carry meaning. Splitting every 500 characters regardless of structure cuts sentences and separates a rule from its exception. Better approaches split along headings and paragraphs, keep a little overlap, and store the document title and section path with each chunk so a passage still makes sense out of context. Chunk size is a tuning parameter, and the right value depends on your documents, which is why evaluation matters.

### 3. Indexing and retrieval

Most teams begin with **vector search**, which finds passages with similar meaning. It handles paraphrases well but can miss exact terms such as product codes, clause numbers or names. **Keyword search** (BM25) is the opposite. **Hybrid retrieval**, combining both, is a strong default for business content. A **reranker** then reorders the top candidates with a more precise model, which often improves quality noticeably for a modest latency cost. Metadata filters narrow the search, for instance to a department or document type.

### 4. Query handling

Users write vague, conversational questions: "what about for contractors?" A rewriting step can turn a follow-up into a standalone query using conversation history. For complex questions, the system may split them into sub-questions or choose among several indexes.

### 5. Generation and grounding

The prompt instructs the model to answer only from the provided passages, cite them, say when the evidence is insufficient and follow your tone and format rules. Citations should link to the actual document and section so users can verify. When retrieved evidence is weak, a well-designed assistant says "I could not find this in the documents" and offers a human contact, which builds more trust than a plausible guess.

### 6. Access control

This is where many enterprise projects get into trouble. If your index mixes HR files, board papers and public manuals, the assistant must only retrieve what the signed-in user may read. Carry permissions into the index as metadata and filter at query time, and never rely on the model to "not mention" restricted content once it has been placed in the prompt.

### 7. Observability

Log questions, retrieved passages, answers and user feedback, with appropriate privacy controls. These logs reveal missing documents, confusing content, unanswered questions and failure patterns, and they are the raw material for improving the system after launch.

## Evaluation: the part teams skip

You cannot improve what you cannot measure, and "it looked good in the demo" is not measurement. Build an evaluation set before tuning:

- Collect **real questions** from the intended users, including awkward, ambiguous and out-of-scope ones.
- Write **reference answers** or mark which documents should be retrieved.
- Score **retrieval**: did the right passage appear in the top results?
- Score **faithfulness**: is every claim in the answer supported by the retrieved text?
- Score **answer quality** and **refusal behaviour**: does it decline questions it should not answer?
- Rerun the set on every change to prompts, chunking, models or retrieval settings, so you see regressions immediately.

Automated judging with a second model can scale scoring, but calibrate it against human review on a sample, because automated judges have their own biases. A few hundred well-chosen questions beat thousands of random ones.

## Common mistakes we see

**Treating it as a prompt problem.** Most failures originate in ingestion and retrieval. Fix those before rewriting the prompt for the tenth time.

**Indexing everything.** Outdated, duplicate and draft documents poison answers. Curate the corpus and keep owners responsible for freshness.

**No refusal path.** Without an instruction and an evaluation case for "I do not know", the model will improvise.

**Ignoring permissions.** A demo on public documents does not reveal the access-control problem you will meet on day one of the real rollout.

**Skipping security testing.** RAG introduces **indirect prompt injection**: a malicious instruction hidden in a document or web page the assistant retrieves can attempt to hijack it. Combined with tools, it can cause data leakage or unwanted actions. Test for it. Our [AI security and governance service](/services/ai-security-governance) covers it, and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications is a good starting list for any build team.

**Chasing the biggest model.** A larger model costs more and responds slower. Often better retrieval and a mid-sized model give a better answer at lower cost. Measure on your own questions.

**No plan for ownership.** Someone must own the content, the evaluation set and the monthly review. Without that, quality decays quietly.

## A staged path from idea to production

1. **Scope one use case** with a clear audience, such as the IT helpdesk or HR policy questions.
2. **Collect questions and documents** and agree what "good" means.
3. **Build the pipeline and the evaluation set** together.
4. **Pilot with a small group**, capturing feedback on every answer.
5. **Review results**, fix retrieval and content gaps, add guardrails and security testing.
6. **Expand deliberately**, adding sources and audiences once metrics hold.

A pilot like this commonly takes a few weeks for a clean, well-bounded document set, and longer when documents are messy or permissions are complex. It is also a legitimate outcome to learn the use case is not worth scaling.

## Data protection and governance

If documents contain personal data, the DPDP Act's expectations around purpose, consent, security safeguards and retention apply to your assistant as to any system. Decide what may be indexed, how long conversation logs are kept, who can read them and which model providers may process the content, and document it.
