Searching for VAPT services in Hyderabad usually produces a long list of vendors, similar-looking packages and very little explanation of what you will actually receive. This guide explains what vulnerability assessment and penetration testing (VAPT) includes, how to scope it, what a good report looks like and the questions that separate careful testers from report factories. It is written for founders, CTOs and engineering managers buying a test, many of them for the first time.

## VA versus PT: what the acronym hides

VAPT bundles two activities that are often confused.

A **vulnerability assessment (VA)** looks broadly for known weaknesses. It relies heavily on scanners that check software versions, misconfigurations and common flaws, and then a human removes false positives. It answers: what known problems exist across this estate?

A **penetration test (PT)** is goal-driven and mostly manual. A tester behaves like an attacker: they chain small weaknesses, bypass controls, abuse business logic and try to reach something valuable, such as another customer's data or administrative control. It answers: what could an attacker actually do, and how bad would it be?

You want both. The assessment gives breadth, the penetration test gives depth, and a good provider explains which findings came from which method. If a quotation lists only "scan" activities and calls it a penetration test, ask what the human will do.

## The four common scopes

### Web application and API testing

This is the most common request. Testers examine authentication and session management, access control, input handling, business logic, file upload and download, third-party integrations and client-side behaviour. For APIs they test object-level authorisation, mass assignment, rate limiting and excessive data exposure. A great deal of real-world breach risk sits in broken access control: changing an identifier in a request and seeing someone else's record. Scanners rarely find it, which is why manual testing matters.

### Mobile application testing

Android and iOS apps are tested for insecure data storage, weak transport security and certificate pinning, exposed components, hard-coded secrets, tampering and reverse-engineering resistance, and, critically, the API behind the app. Many mobile findings are really server findings reached through the app.

### Network and infrastructure testing

External testing looks at what an attacker on the internet can reach: exposed services, outdated software, weak credentials and misconfigured firewalls. Internal testing assumes an attacker already has a foothold, for example through phishing, and examines segmentation, privilege escalation and, in Windows environments, Active Directory attack paths.

### Cloud configuration and security testing

Cloud environments fail through configuration, not just code: overly permissive identity roles, public storage buckets, exposed management ports, secrets in repositories and missing logging. Testing reviews identity and access management, network rules, storage, key management, container and Kubernetes settings and audit logging against provider benchmarks.

A fifth option, **secure code review**, complements these. Reviewers read the critical paths, such as authentication, payments and data access, and find issues dynamic testing may miss, with exact line references for developers.

## Black box, grey box, white box

| Approach | What the tester gets | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Black box | Only the target, as an outsider | Seeing external exposure |
| Grey box | Test accounts for each role, documentation | Most applications, best value |
| White box | Source code, architecture and credentials | Critical systems, deepest coverage |

For a typical SaaS application, grey box testing with accounts for at least two tenants and an administrator role is a strong default, because it lets testers probe cross-tenant access, the issue customers worry about most.

## A realistic testing process

1. **Scoping call.** The vendor asks what the system does, who uses it, what data it holds, which environments exist and what is off limits. Be wary of a fixed price quoted without any questions.
2. **Rules of engagement.** A signed document covering targets, dates, source IP addresses, test accounts, forbidden actions such as denial of service, escalation contacts and data handling.
3. **Reconnaissance and mapping.** Enumerating endpoints, technologies, roles and trust boundaries.
4. **Testing.** Automated scanning plus manual attack on each function, following references such as the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide and ASVS for web, OWASP MASVS and MASTG for mobile, and PTES and NIST SP 800-115 for infrastructure.
5. **Exploitation and impact validation.** Confirming that issues are real, proving impact safely and chaining findings.
6. **Reporting and walkthrough.** A written report, and ideally a session where testers explain findings to your developers.
7. **Retest.** Verifying that fixes work.

## What a good report contains

A useful report is something your developers can act on the same day. Look for:

- An **executive summary** that states overall risk in plain language and highlights the two or three issues that matter most.
- For each finding: affected asset, **step-by-step reproduction**, evidence such as requests, responses or screenshots, impact explained in business terms, a severity rating with reasoning (CVSS plus context) and specific remediation advice.
- A **scope and methodology** section stating what was tested, what was not and any limitations such as time boxes or blocked tests.
- A statement of **what was verified manually**, so you know how much was scanner output.
- A **retest section** or closure letter showing which findings were fixed.

Warning signs include reports padded with raw scanner output, identical text across different clients, severity inflation, no reproduction steps and "recommendations" that just say "apply security best practices".

## How long it takes and what drives price

Most providers, including us, price by effort, so the factors are the same everywhere: number of applications and user roles, number of API endpoints, mobile platforms, network size, depth of testing (grey versus white box), retesting needs and reporting requirements such as customer-specific formats. A small web application can be tested in about a week; large or multi-tenant platforms take several weeks. We do not publish prices because the range is too wide to be honest, but a good vendor will give you a fixed quote, with assumptions listed, after a scoping call.

## Questions to ask any VAPT vendor

1. Who will actually test, and what experience do they have with systems like mine?
2. How much of the work is manual, and can you show a sample report with sensitive details removed?
3. What methodology do you follow and how do you rate severity?
4. How do you protect our data and test accounts during the engagement?
5. Is retesting included, and for how long after the report?
6. Will developers get a walkthrough, and can they ask questions afterwards?
7. If a regulator or customer requires a particular format, or testing by an auditor on a specific empanelment list, can you meet it? Some regulated sectors in India require testers from lists maintained by bodies such as CERT-In, so check your obligations before choosing.
8. What do you refuse to do? A tester who says yes to everything may not be careful.

## Preparing for a test

Preparation reduces cost and improves findings. Make sure staging mirrors production, create test accounts for each role and tenant, prepare API documentation or a Postman collection, disable or whitelist rate limiters and web application firewalls where agreed, take backups, tell your hosting provider and security monitoring team about the test window, and nominate a developer to answer questions quickly. Share your own worries as well, such as account takeover, data export abuse or payment tampering, so testers prioritise them.

## After the report: making it count

Triage findings with developers within a week. Fix critical and high issues first, and treat repeating patterns, for example missing authorisation checks, as design problems rather than individual bugs. Add regression tests for fixed issues, ask for the retest, and keep the closure letter for customer questionnaires and for auditors if you are working toward ISO 27001 or SOC 2. Then ask what would have caught these issues earlier: code review checklists, dependency scanning in your pipeline, or developer training.

Testing once a year is a snapshot. Between tests, monitoring and good engineering habits keep risk down.
