## The problem: AI demos that fall apart in production

It is easy to build an impressive chatbot demo in a weekend. It is much harder to build one that gives correct answers on your real documents, respects who may see what, handles questions it should refuse, survives adversarial users and keeps working when the model or the data changes. Teams that skip the hard parts end up with an assistant nobody trusts, or worse, one that leaks something it should not.

We build AI assistants and agents as software systems with testing, monitoring and security, not as prompts pasted into a model.

## What we build

**Knowledge assistants (RAG).** A chat or search interface over policies, product documentation, contracts, tickets, wikis or PDFs. Answers cite the passages they used so users can verify them.

**Workflow agents.** Assistants that act: triage support emails and draft replies, extract data from invoices into your ERP, qualify inbound leads, prepare weekly reports, or route internal requests. Each action runs under explicit permissions with approval steps where risk is high.

**Copilots inside your product.** Natural-language search, summarisation or guided workflows embedded in your existing application, built with the same engineering discipline as the rest of your codebase.

## Workflow automation and analytics integration

Not every AI project needs a chat window. Many of the best returns come from quiet automation: classifying inbound emails and routing them, extracting fields from invoices and contracts into your ERP, drafting weekly reports from your own data, or flagging anomalies for a human to review. We build these as ordinary, testable workflows with a language model as one step, not as a black box.

Analytics integration works the same way. Your dashboards and warehouse already hold the answers to many questions people currently ask an analyst. We connect assistants to governed, read-only views of that data, so someone can ask "which accounts renewed late last quarter?" and receive a sourced answer with the query shown, rather than a guess. Access is scoped by role, results are logged, and anything that would change data goes through an approval step.

## Our approach

1. **Define the job and the failure modes.** We write down the questions or tasks, who asks, what a good answer looks like and what the assistant must refuse. This becomes the specification.
2. **Prepare the knowledge.** Documents are cleaned, chunked sensibly, enriched with metadata and indexed with hybrid retrieval, combining keyword and semantic search, because pure vector search misses exact terms such as part numbers and clause references.
3. **Build the evaluation set first.** We collect real questions, write reference answers and score retrieval quality, answer faithfulness and refusal behaviour. Without this, you cannot tell whether a change made things better.
4. **Build, then tighten.** Prompting, retrieval tuning, reranking, tool design and guardrails are iterated against the evaluation set, not by gut feel.
5. **Secure it.** We design against prompt injection, data leakage and excessive agency from the start, and offer independent testing through our [AI security and governance](/services/ai-security-governance) service.
6. **Deploy and observe.** Logging, cost tracking, feedback buttons and dashboards show how the assistant performs and where it fails, so it improves after launch.

## Tools and technology

We are provider-agnostic and select models by accuracy, latency, cost and data-residency needs, including hosted frontier models and open-weight models you run yourself. Typical components include a vector or hybrid search store such as PostgreSQL with pgvector, OpenSearch or a managed vector database, document parsing for PDF, Office and scanned files, orchestration code written as plain, testable application logic, and evaluation tooling for retrieval and answer quality. Integrations cover email, Slack and Teams, WhatsApp Business, CRMs, ticketing tools and internal APIs.

## What a pilot looks like

A typical pilot covers one use case, one audience and one document collection, for example an internal policy assistant for HR and IT queries. In the first fortnight we gather questions and documents and agree the evaluation set. By week four there is a working assistant for a small group of real users, with feedback captured on every answer. By week six you have evaluation scores, a list of failure patterns, cost per conversation and a recommendation: expand, change course or stop. Stopping is a legitimate outcome, and finding that out cheaply is the point of a pilot.
